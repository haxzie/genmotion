Most "product launch video examples" articles are a list of videos with adjectives attached. Watching twelve polished films teaches you very little, because what makes them work isn't visible on the surface — it's structural, and it's format-dependent.

So this is organised the other way round: by the six formats that actually get made, what each one is structurally for, and how the beats are allocated inside it. Real examples are embedded throughout.

If you haven't read it yet, the [7-beat blueprint](/blog/how-to-make-a-product-launch-video) is the underlying structure everything here is a variation on — open on the dream, hit the wall, agitate, reveal the product, name three capabilities, give proof, land the tagline.

## 1. The teaser

**Runtime:** 10–20 seconds
**Job:** create intrigue before you have anything to explain
**Beats used:** 1 and 7 only

The teaser is the format people get wrong most often, because the instinct is to compress the full story into fifteen seconds. That produces a trailer for a trailer — breathless and unmemorable.

A teaser should do exactly two things: gesture at the ambition (beat 1) and land a tagline or date (beat 7). Everything in between is deliberately withheld. No feature list, no explanation, ideally not even a clear statement of what the product is.

**Structure:**

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0–3s | One striking visual or line — the aspiration, not the product |
| 3–12s | Build. Motion escalates, information doesn't |
| 12–18s | Product name, tagline, date |

**The test:** if a viewer could accurately describe what your product does after watching, it's not a teaser — it's a short reveal, and it'll underperform both.

## 2. The full reveal

**Runtime:** 45–90 seconds
**Job:** the flagship launch film — the one on your landing page and your Product Hunt post
**Beats used:** all seven

This is the format the 7-beat blueprint was written for, and the one worth spending real effort on because everything else can be cut from it.

The most common failure is starting at beat 4. Founders are close to their product and want to introduce it in the first five seconds, which skips the part that makes anyone care. Beats 1 through 3 exist to make the reveal land — you're establishing what the viewer wants, naming the obstacle, and making them feel it before you offer the answer.

The second most common failure is naming five or six capabilities. Three is not a stylistic preference; it's the number people retain.

::video https://youtu.be/PA0mjzzhtVU "A product overview built as a full reveal — GenMotion explaining itself end to end."

**Structure at 60 seconds:**

| Time | Beat |
| --- | --- |
| 0–8s | Open on the dream |
| 8–12s | Hit the wall |
| 12–22s | Agitate |
| 22–28s | Reveal the product |
| 28–45s | Three capabilities |
| 45–52s | Proof |
| 52–55s | Tagline |

**Watch for:** whether the first five seconds mention the product at all. In the strongest examples, they don't.

## 3. The feature launch

**Runtime:** 20–40 seconds
**Job:** announce one capability to people who already know the product
**Beats used:** 2, 4, 5 (one capability), 7

This is the format you'll make most often, and it needs a different structure from a launch because your audience is different. They already understand the category and the product. Beats 1 and 3 — establishing the dream and agitating the problem — are wasted on them.

Instead: name the specific friction, show the new thing removing it, land the name.

::video https://youtu.be/yKRFiScfz28 "A feature launch — kinetic type, tight pacing, and one clear hero moment."

**Structure at 30 seconds:**

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0–5s | The specific friction, stated concretely |
| 5–20s | The feature, doing the thing, as the hero moment |
| 20–27s | What it means for them — outcome, not mechanism |
| 27–30s | Name and availability |

**Watch for:** one hero moment that the whole piece is built around. Feature videos that show three things at once show nothing.

## 4. The data story

**Runtime:** 15–30 seconds
**Job:** make a number feel like an event
**Beats used:** 6, expanded

A milestone, a benchmark, a funding round, a usage stat. This format is almost pure beat 6 — proof — stretched into a piece of its own.

It's also the format with the highest ratio of impact to effort, because a single animated number is genuinely enough. The mistake is decorating it: surrounding a good number with charts, logos and supporting stats dilutes the one thing you wanted people to remember.

::video https://youtu.be/geKsRvFBVG4 "A data story built around a single animated number — a GitHub star milestone."

**Structure at 20 seconds:**

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0–4s | Context — what the number is about |
| 4–14s | The count-up. This is the whole video |
| 14–20s | What it means, then the mark |

**Watch for:** restraint. One number, counted up with a proper ease, held long enough to register.

## 5. The event promo

**Runtime:** 20–40 seconds
**Job:** drive registrations
**Beats used:** 1, 4, 6, 7 — plus logistics

Event promos have a requirement the other formats don't: they must communicate concrete details — date, place, who's speaking — without turning into a slide. The tension is that logistics are boring and the video's job is to make attendance feel urgent.

The resolution is to treat the details as a rhythmic beat rather than an information dump: date, location and one name, each landing on its own moment with the same weight as a headline.

::video https://youtu.be/D_3coLrObj8 "An event promo — motion-driven energy built to fill a single frame and drive sign-ups."

**Structure at 30 seconds:**

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0–6s | The reason to care — the ambition, not the agenda |
| 6–18s | Energy and identity. What kind of event this is |
| 18–26s | The specifics, one per beat |
| 26–30s | Where to register |

**Watch for:** whether the frame ever stops moving. Event promos live or die on pace.

## 6. The founder announcement

**Runtime:** 60–120 seconds
**Job:** trade polish for credibility
**Beats used:** loosely 1–7, spoken rather than designed

A person, a camera, an honest explanation. This is the anti-format, and it's genuinely effective in the right context. A maker on Product Hunt put the argument for it well:

> "It feels human. It creates a connection that polished animations rarely do."

The trade-off is real. It doesn't scale, it can't be cut down for social, and it puts your delivery on screen. It also can't be redone cheaply when the product changes.

The strongest use is hybrid: the founder speaking, with motion-graphic titles, callouts and stat cards layered over the top. You keep the credibility and add the clarity that talking heads lack.

**Watch for:** whether the person is actually explaining or performing. The format only works when it's the former.

## Which format should you make?

| Situation | Format |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-launch, building anticipation | Teaser |
| Launch day, Product Hunt, landing page hero | Full reveal |
| Shipping something to existing users | Feature launch |
| Milestone, benchmark or funding news | Data story |
| Conference, webinar, meetup | Event promo |
| Small audience, high-trust, no budget | Founder announcement |

The efficient sequence is to build the full reveal first and derive the rest from it. Its opening becomes the teaser. Each capability beat becomes a feature launch. Its proof beat becomes a data story. Its strongest ten seconds becomes the social cut.

One caveat worth planning around: derive the *cutdowns* before you animate, not after. Reframing a finished 16:9 composition for 9:16 means recomposing each scene, not cropping it — the [aspect ratio calculator](/tools/aspect-ratio-calculator) and [social video size guide](/tools/social-video-size-guide) have the target dimensions.

## What to actually take from examples

Three questions are worth more than an hour of watching:

**What do the first five seconds do?** In the videos that work, they establish something the viewer wants — not what the product is. Count how many seconds pass before the product is named.

**How many things does it claim?** Almost always three, or one. Videos claiming six are usually the ones you can't remember afterwards.

**Does it earn its runtime?** A 90-second video should have a reason to be 90 seconds. Most 90-second launch videos are 45-second videos with the pacing slowed down.

And one thing not to take: the styling. Copying a video's look without its structure is how you end up with something polished and unpersuasive. Structure is transferable; aesthetics belong to the brand that built them.
